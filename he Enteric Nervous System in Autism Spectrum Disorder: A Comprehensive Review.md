## ABSTRACT

**Background:** Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental condition characterized by difficulties in communication, social interaction, and repetitive behaviors. Beyond its core neurological features, ASD is frequently accompanied by gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms that significantly impact quality of life. Emerging research suggests that these GI issues may be intrinsically linked to the disorder through deficits in the enteric nervous system (ENS)—the "second brain" of the gut.

**Objective:** This comprehensive review examines the structural and functional abnormalities in the ENS observed in ASD, the genetic and environmental factors contributing to these deficits, the underlying mechanisms connecting ENS dysfunction to ASD symptomatology, and the therapeutic implications of targeting the gut–brain axis.

**Methods:** A systematic review of peer-reviewed literature published between 2000 and 2026 was conducted, focusing on studies examining ENS structure and function, genetic risk factors for ASD with ENS expression, environmental factors affecting the ENS, and therapeutic interventions targeting the gut–brain axis in ASD.

**Results:** Structural abnormalities in the ENS of individuals with ASD include reduced neuronal density, altered neuronal populations, and enteric glial dysfunction. Functional impairments include dysmotility, impaired secretion, compromised intestinal barrier integrity, and visceral hypersensitivity. ASD-associated genes (SHANK3, NLGN3, CNTNAP2, SLC6A4, CHD8, TCF4, FOXP1, MET) are expressed in both the brain and the ENS, suggesting that the genetic architecture of ASD may simultaneously affect both systems. Environmental factors—including maternal immune activation, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and zinc deficiency—further compound these deficits. Multiple mechanisms link ENS dysfunction to ASD, including serotonergic signaling disruption, inhibitory pathway dysfunction, GABA receptor modulation, enteric glial dysfunction, and excitatory/inhibitory imbalance. Therapeutic strategies targeting the gut–brain axis, including microbiome-targeted therapies, dietary interventions, zinc supplementation, and neuroprotective approaches, show promise.

**Conclusion:** The enteric nervous system plays a significant role in ASD pathophysiology. Understanding the ENS–ASD connection opens new avenues for earlier diagnosis, subtype identification, and novel therapeutic strategies. Further research is needed to establish causality, identify biomarkers, and develop targeted interventions.

**Keywords:** autism spectrum disorder, enteric nervous system, gut–brain axis, gastrointestinal dysfunction, genetic risk factors, gut microbiota, SHANK3, serotonin, neurodevelopment, therapeutic intervention

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental condition characterized by difficulties in communication and social interaction, as well as repetitive behaviors or restricted interests (Kanner, 1968; Volkmar et al., 2014). It represents a growing public health concern, with an estimated prevalence of 1:59 among children and 1:100 among adults (Wang et al., 2023). Despite decades of research, the etiology of ASD remains incompletely understood, with both genetic and environmental factors contributing to its pathogenesis.

Beyond the core symptoms, ASD patients experience various comorbidities that significantly impact their quality of life. Among the most prevalent and clinically significant are gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (Matson and Goldin, 2013). Research has consistently demonstrated that individuals with autism frequently experience elevated occurrences of diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, and constipation compared to the general population (Horvath et al., 1999; Wasilewska and Klukowski, 2015). These GI issues are not merely inconvenient; they correlate with the severity of core ASD behaviors, including social withdrawal, irritability, and anxiety (Chaidez et al., 2014; Madra et al., 2020).

The connection between gut dysfunction and brain development is mediated by the enteric nervous system (ENS), often referred to as the "second brain." The ENS is a vast, complex network of approximately 500 million neurons embedded in the walls of the gastrointestinal tract. It operates with significant autonomy from the central nervous system (CNS) while maintaining constant bidirectional communication through the gut–brain axis.

This review provides a comprehensive examination of:
1. The structural and functional organization of the ENS
2. The specific deficits observed in the ENS in ASD
3. Genetic risk factors for ASD that affect both the brain and the ENS
4. Environmental factors that influence ENS development and function in ASD
5. The mechanisms linking ENS dysfunction to ASD symptomatology
6. Clinical and therapeutic implications of targeting the ENS in ASD

## 2. THE ENTERIC NERVOUS SYSTEM: STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

### 2.1 Anatomical Organization

The ENS is a vital component of the autonomic nervous system that spans the entire length of the gastrointestinal tract. It consists of a complex network of neurons and glia organized into two principal ganglionated plexuses in mammals:

**2.1.1 Myenteric (Auerbach's) Plexus**

Located between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers, the myenteric plexus governs intestinal contractile patterns and circuits that synchronize these patterns with other intestinal behaviors. It predominantly houses the neurons that regulate intestinal motility.

**2.1.2 Submucosal (Meissner's) Plexus**

Situated between the muscularis mucosa and circular muscle, the submucosal plexus contains neurons projecting to the mucosa involved in regulating secretion, vasodilation, and absorption.

### 2.2 Cellular Composition

**2.2.1 Enteric Neurons**

Enteric neurons comprise a diverse population of cells, encompassing:
- **Intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs):** Detect mechanical or chemical stimulation of the intestinal wall
- **Ascending interneurons:** Project orally and activate excitatory motor neurons
- **Descending interneurons:** Project anally and activate inhibitory motor neurons
- **Excitatory motor neurons:** Activate contraction of circular and longitudinal muscles via acetylcholine and substance P
- **Inhibitory motor neurons:** Activate relaxation of circular and longitudinal muscles via nitric oxide, VIP, and ATP
- **Vasodilator neurons:** Regulate blood flow
- **Secretomotor neurons:** Regulate secretion of water and electrolytes

**2.2.2 Enteric Glial Cells (EGCs)**

Enteric glial cells, the non-neuronal components of the ENS, display diverse morphologies and are situated in distinct microenvironments:
- **Protoplasmic type I glia:** Located within enteric ganglia
- **Fibrous type II glia:** Found along fiber tracts
- **Subepithelial type III glia:** Positioned near the epithelium
- **Intramuscular type IV glia:** Dispersed throughout smooth muscle layers

Emerging research has challenged the traditional characterization of EGCs as merely supportive cells. They are now recognized as active participants in modulating synaptic signaling, regulating gut motility patterns, maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, and interacting with epithelial and immune cells (Seguella and Gulbransen, 2021).

### 2.3 Extrinsic Innervation

The ENS is also innervated by extrinsic nerves, including:
- **Vagal nerves:** Primarily parasympathetic, providing sensory input and modulating ENS activity
- **Spinal nerves:** Sympathetic, modulating motility and secretion

### 2.4 Functional Roles

To maintain whole-body homeostasis, the GI tract generates various patterns of motility:
- **Peristalsis:** Coordinated contraction and relaxation propelling contents forward
- **Accommodation:** Relaxation of the proximal stomach to receive food
- **Mixing:** Segmentation contractions that mix and break down food
- **Emptying:** Organized contractions moving contents toward the anus

Accumulating evidence has established a consensus that the ENS serves as the primary initiator of intricate motility patterns (Huizinga et al., 2011; Schneider et al., 2019). The ENS can generate complex motor patterns without any input from the central nervous system, demonstrating its functional autonomy.

### 2.5 The Peristaltic Reflex

The peristaltic reflex is a classic gastrointestinal motility pattern coordinated by a five-neuron unit:
1. Intrinsic sensory neuron (IPAN): Detects mechanical or chemical stimuli from the intestinal lumen
2. Ascending interneuron: Transmits signals orally
3. Descending interneuron: Transmits signals anally
4. Excitatory motor neuron: Activates contraction of circular muscle orally via acetylcholine
5. Inhibitory motor neuron: Activates relaxation of circular muscle anally via nitric oxide and VIP

This coordinated activity results in an oral contraction and distal relaxation, propelling contents in an aboral direction.

## 3. THE GUT–BRAIN AXIS

### 3.1 Definition

The gut–brain axis is a bidirectional communication network connecting the central nervous system (CNS) with the enteric nervous system (ENS) and the gastrointestinal tract. This axis integrates neural, endocrine, and immune signaling pathways.

### 3.2 Components

| Component | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **Vagus Nerve** | The primary neural pathway linking the brainstem to the ENS; ~80-90% of fibers are afferent (sensory) |
| **Endocrine Pathways** | Hormones released from the GI tract (e.g., ghrelin, GLP-1, CCK) affect brain function; stress hormones (cortisol) affect gut function |
| **Immune System** | Cytokines and inflammatory mediators communicate between gut and brain; immune cells in the gut respond to microbial signals |
| **Gut Microbiota** | Produce neurotransmitters (serotonin, GABA, dopamine), metabolites (SCFAs), and signaling molecules that affect CNS function |
| **Enteric Nervous System** | Processes sensory information and generates motor responses; communicates with the CNS via vagal and spinal pathways |

### 3.3 Neurotransmitters and Signaling Molecules

| Molecule | Source | Function |
|----------|--------|----------|
| **Serotonin (5-HT)** | Enteroendocrine cells (~90% of body's 5-HT), enteric neurons | Regulates motility, secretion, peristalsis; affects mood, cognition |
| **Dopamine** | Enteric neurons, gut microbiota | Regulates motility; affects reward, motivation |
| **GABA** | Enteric neurons, gut microbiota | Modulates enteric neuron excitability; affects anxiety, stress |
| **Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs)** | Microbial fermentation (acetate, propionate, butyrate) | Energy source for colonocytes; regulate intestinal barrier; affect brain via receptors and epigenetic mechanisms |
| **Nitric Oxide (NO)** | Inhibitory motor neurons | Relaxes smooth muscle; neurotransmitter |
| **Acetylcholine (ACh)** | Excitatory motor neurons | Contracts smooth muscle; neurotransmitter |
| **Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide (VIP)** | Inhibitory motor neurons | Relaxes smooth muscle; regulates secretion |

## 4. ENS DEFICITS IN AUTISM SPECTRUM DISORDER

### 4.1 Structural Abnormalities

Research has identified several structural abnormalities in the ENS of individuals with ASD:

**4.1.1 Reduced Neuronal Density**
- Decreased number of enteric neurons in both myenteric and submucosal plexuses
- Reduced colonization by postmitotic enteric neurons in the GI tract (observed in CHD8 zebrafish models)
- ENS hypoplasia (observed in SERT Ala56 mouse models)

**4.1.2 Altered Neuronal Populations**
- Increased nNOS+ (nitrergic) neurons in the small intestine (observed in NL3R451C mice)
- Unchanged nNOS or calretinin motor neurons in some models (Nlgn3-/- mice)
- Imbalances in excitatory and inhibitory neuron ratios

**4.1.3 Enteric Glial Dysfunction**
- Altered glial cell numbers and morphology
- Impaired glial support and signaling
- Disrupted interactions with epithelial and immune cells

**4.1.4 Altered Synaptic Organization**
- Planar cell polarity gene mutations affecting connectivity (Sasselli et al., 2013)
- Abnormal neuronal projections

### 4.2 Functional Impairments

The structural changes translate directly into functional impairments:

**4.2.1 Dysmotility**
- Reduced peristalsis frequency and prolonged transit time (Shank3 zebrafish models)
- Faster small intestinal transit (NL3R451C mice)
- Faster colonic motility (Nlgn3-/- mice, Caspr2-/- mice)
- Altered colonic contractions (Caspr2-/- mice)
- Impaired colonic contractility (Foxp1-/- mice)

**4.2.2 Impaired Secretion and Absorption**
- Altered fluid and electrolyte balance
- Reduced serotonin-positive enteroendocrine cells (Shank3 models)

**4.2.3 Compromised Intestinal Barrier Integrity**
- Elevated levels of zonulin-1 (a marker of intestinal permeability)
- Increased IL-6 levels
- Altered GI morphology

**4.2.4 Altered Microbial Composition**
- Dysbiosis in ASD patients and animal models
- Changes in bacterial diversity and abundance

**4.2.5 Visceral Hypersensitivity**
- Chronic gut pain (observed in NL3R451C patients and mice)
- Increased pain perception from normal gut activity

## 5. GENETIC RISK FACTORS

### 5.1 Overview

Although genetic risk is a major contributor to idiopathic ASD, the specific genetic alterations underlying most cases have yet to be identified. Many ASD-associated genes encode proteins involved in synapse formation and maintenance. Notably, some of these mutations have also been found to be expressed in the ENS, suggesting that the genetic architecture of ASD may simultaneously affect both neurological and gastrointestinal function.

### 5.2 SHANK3

**Gene Location:** Mouse chromosome 15E3; human location 22q13.3

**Protein Function:** Encodes a scaffolding protein in the postsynaptic density involved in clustering or assembling receptors, adhesion molecules, and signaling molecules (Jiang and Ehlers, 2013).

**ASD Association:** Haploinsufficiency causes Phelan–McDermid syndrome (PMS), which frequently manifests with autistic or autistic-like behavior among patients (Phelan and McDermid, 2012; Sarasua et al., 2014).

**GI Symptoms in Patients:** Gastroesophageal reflux, cyclical vomiting, diarrhea, and/or constipation (Phelan and McDermid, 2012; Soorya et al., 2013).

**ENS Effects in Animal Models:**

| Model | Finding | Reference |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| Shank3 KO zebrafish | Abnormal GI transit and motility due to reductions in serotonin-positive enteroendocrine cells; reduced peristalsis frequency and prolonged transit time | James et al., 2019 |
| Shank3 KO mice | Altered GI morphology and microbiota composition; elevated levels of zonulin-1 and IL-6; decreased Shank3 gene expression in gut epithelial cells | Sauer et al., 2019 |

**Mechanism:** SHANK3 mutations affect serotonin-positive enteroendocrine cells, which are involved in subsequent secretion and peristalsis-associated neural pathways.

**Clinical Significance:** The presence of SHANK3 expression in the GI epithelium suggests that extracerebral factors contribute to the phenotype observed in SHANK3-deficient individuals (Sauer et al., 2019).

### 5.3 Neuroligin-3 (NLGN3)

**Gene Location:** X chromosome

**Protein Function:** Postsynaptic cell-adhesion molecule that interacts with presynaptic partners (neurexins) and postsynaptic scaffolding proteins to facilitate synaptic maturation and transmission (Krueger et al., 2012).

**ASD Association:** R451C mutation (arginine-to-cysteine substitution at the 451st amino acid residue) identified in ASD patients (Jamain et al., 2003; Hosie et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022).

**GI Symptoms in Patients:** Diarrhea and chronic gut pain (Hosie et al., 2019).

**ENS Effects in Animal Models:**

| Model | Finding | Reference |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| NL3R451C knock-in mice | Increased Hu and nNOS myenteric neurons in the small intestine; faster small intestinal transit; heightened sensitivity to GABA receptor modulation; unchanged colonic motility | Hosie et al., 2019 |
| Nlgn3 KO mice | Unchanged nNOS or calretinin motor neurons; faster colonic motility; enlarged colonic diameter | Leembruggen et al., 2020 |

**Mechanism:** NLGN3 exerts a modulatory effect on neural pathways within the ENS, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of GI disorders in ASD patients with NLGN3 mutations.

### 5.4 Caspr2 (CNTNAP2)

**Gene Location:** Chromosome 7q35

**Protein Function:** Member of the Neurexin family that acts as a synaptic cell-adhesion molecule involved in forming trans-synaptic complexes (Alarcon et al., 2008; Penagarikano and Geschwind, 2012).

**ASD Association:** The first gene discovered to have rare and common mutations that increase susceptibility to ASD; associated with language regression, paresthesia, and social impairment (Strauss et al., 2006; Penagarikano et al., 2011; Penagarikano and Geschwind, 2012).

**ENS Effects in Animal Models:**

| Model | Finding | Reference |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| Caspr2-/- mice | Unchanged organization of neurons within the myenteric plexus; Caspr2 expressed in enteric sensory neurons in both the small intestine and colon; no significant difference in whole GI transit time; altered colonic contractions and accelerated colonic motility | Robinson et al., 2023 |

**Mechanism:** Caspr2 expression in enteric sensory neurons suggests that enteric sensory dysfunction may contribute to ASD-related GI symptoms.

### 5.5 SLC6A4 (Serotonin Transporter)

**Gene Location:** Chromosome 17q11.2

**Protein Function:** Encodes the serotonin (5-HT) reuptake transporter (SERT), which regulates extracellular serotonin levels (Wade et al., 1996; Gershon, 2013; Homberg et al., 2013).

**ASD Association:** Rare mutations in multiple alleles cause hyperserotoninemia; elevated whole-blood serotonin is a biomarker in ~30% of ASD children (Schain and Freedman, 1961; Sutcliffe et al., 2005; Veenstra-VanderWeele et al., 2012; Gabriele et al., 2014; Muller et al., 2016).

**GI Symptoms in Patients:** Whole-blood serotonin levels associated with lower GI symptoms (abdominal pain, stool retention, and large bowel movements); functional constipation—the most frequent complaint—is not associated (Marler et al., 2015).

**ENS Effects in Animal Models:**

| Model | Finding | Reference |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| SERT Ala56 knock-in mice | Decreased enteric neurons; increased 5-HT clearance; ASD-like behavior; slow GI transit; diminished peristaltic reflex activity; increased proliferation of crypt epithelial cells | Margolis et al., 2016 |

**Mechanism:** Alterations in SERT-mediated 5-HT clearance can impact all ENS functions mediated by 5-HT. The serotonergic signaling pathway—including alterations in serotonin-positive enteroendocrine cells and SERT activity—may impact 5-HT signaling that regulates enteric neuronal development and gastrointestinal motility.

**Clinical Significance:** Investigating the role of 5-HT and SERT in brain–gut disorders may offer valuable insights into ASD treatment.

### 5.6 CHD8

**Gene Location:** Chromosome 14q11.2

**Protein Function:** Chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein 8 involved in transcriptional regulation, epigenetic remodeling, cell proliferation promotion, and RNA synthesis regulation (Thompson et al., 2008; Nishiyama et al., 2012).

**ASD Association:** Initially associated with autism in 2012 (O'Roak et al., 2012a,b); subsequently identified as one of the most recurrently disrupted genes in ASD; associated with macrocephaly, distinctive facial features, and digestive issues (Bernier et al., 2014).

**GI Symptoms in Patients:** Children with CHD8 mutations exhibit significantly higher incidence of constipation (60%) compared to those without the mutation (26%; Li and Zhou, 2016).

**ENS Effects in Animal Models:**

| Model | Finding | Reference |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| CHD8 mutant zebrafish | 50% reduction of enteric neurons; impaired GI motility; macrocephaly resulting from expansion of the forebrain/midbrain | Bernier et al., 2014 |

**Mechanism:** Disruption of CHD8 in zebrafish reduces colonization by postmitotic enteric neurons in the GI tract, leading to impaired gastrointestinal motility.

### 5.7 Additional Susceptibility Genes

| Gene | Function | ASD Symptoms | GI Symptoms | Evidence |
|------|----------|--------------|-------------|----------|
| **TCF4** | Transcription factor | Stereotypic movements, arm flapping, hand biting | Constipation, gastroesophageal reflux | PTHS patients; TCF4 functional deletion mice show reduced gut transit velocities (Grubisic et al., 2015) |
| **FOXP1** | Forkhead box protein P1 | Delays in motor and language milestones, language impairment | Constipation; impaired colonic contractility and prolonged total GI transit | Foxp1-/- mice (Frohlich et al., 2019) |
| **c-MET** | Proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase | Over-representation of Met rs1858830 C allele in ASD individuals | GI conditions; chronic digestive disease, ileo-colonic lymphoid nodular hyperplasia | ASD individuals with GI dysfunction (Campbell et al., 2009; Russo et al., 2009) |

## 6. ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

### 6.1 Overview

In addition to genetic factors, epidemiological studies have indicated that environmental risks play a crucial role in the development of ASD (Bölte et al., 2018). Here we summarize several biological environmental risk factors that alter intestinal characteristics in individuals with autism.

### 6.2 Prenatal Factors

**Maternal Immune Activation (MIA):**

Emerging evidence suggests that prenatal factors play a pivotal role in shaping the composition of gut microbiota. The ASD mouse model induced by MIA exhibits dysbiosis of gut microbiota resembling the clinical features observed in ASD patients (Hsiao et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2017; Tartaglione et al., 2022).

**Mechanism:**
- MIA induces neurodevelopmental abnormalities via IL-17a signaling
- Pre-conceptional microbiota transplantation may ameliorate MIA-induced abnormalities (Lammert et al., 2018)

**Limitations:**
- Limited research investigating the association between prenatal/perinatal factors and gastrointestinal problems involving the ENS in ASD

### 6.3 Gut Microbiota

**Composition:**

The gut microbiota is a complex ecosystem residing in the intestinal tract, consisting of approximately 100 trillion microorganisms, with two bacterial divisions—Bacteroidetes (~20%) and Firmicutes (~70%)—accounting for ~90% of its composition (Eckburg et al., 2005; Qin et al., 2010).

**Functions:**
- Maintaining intestinal homeostasis
- Regulating brain physiology and behavior (Sherwin et al., 2019)
- Producing neurotransmitters and metabolites
- Modulating immune function

**Observations in ASD:**

| Finding | Implication | References |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| Reduced Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio | Altered microbial balance | Strati et al., 2017 |
| Overgrowth of pathogenic strains (e.g., Clostridium, Desulfovibrio) | Correlated with ASD severity and GI disorders | Song et al., 2004; Parracho et al., 2005 |
| Altered SCFA levels (acetate, propionate, butyrate) | Associated with ASD severity | Adams et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2012 |
| Reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria | Impaired intestinal barrier and immune function | Xu et al., 2019 |

**Mechanisms:**
- The ENS is exposed to and engages with the gut microbiota
- The functional maturation of enteric wiring occurs postnatally under the influence of the gut microbiota (Obata and Pachnis, 2016)
- Microbial metabolites affect neurotransmitter synthesis and gut motility
- Dysbiosis may contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction (leaky gut)

**Therapeutic Approaches:**
- Probiotics and prebiotics
- Fecal microbiota transplantation
- Microbiota transfer therapy (Cryan et al., 2019)

**Challenges and Limitations:**
- No specific microbial species has consistently changed across all ASD-related studies
- Controversies persist due to heterogeneity
- Need to consider other variables: sample size, dietary patterns, regional disparities, antibiotic history

### 6.4 Zinc Deficiency

**Importance:**

Zinc is one of the most prominent trace metals, playing a vital role in:
- Neurogenesis
- Neuronal migration and differentiation
- Cognitive development
- Maintaining healthy brain function (Zoroddu et al., 2019)

**Prevalence in ASD:**

Zinc deficiency is significantly more prevalent among individuals with ASD compared to age-matched healthy controls (Grabrucker, 2013; Pfaender and Grabrucker, 2014).

**ENS-Specific Effects:**

**Zinc Transporter 3 (ZnT3):**
- Widely distributed in the ENS of both humans and pigs (Gonkowski et al., 2009; Wojtkiewicz et al., 2012, 2017a,b)
- Plays a crucial role in transporting zinc ions from the cytoplasm into synaptic vesicles
- Co-localized with key neurotransmitters:
  - vAChT (excitatory neurons)
  - nNOS (inhibitory neurons)
  - VIP (vasoactive intestinal peptide)

**Functional Implications:**
- The majority of myenteric neurons expressing ZnT3 exhibit immunoreactivity to vAChT and/or nNOS
- Suggests that ZnT3 and zinc may exert a modulatory effect on both excitatory and inhibitory neurons
- Facilitates gut smooth muscle contraction and relaxation coordination
- Submucosal plexus ZnT3 may play a role in sensory and pain signal transduction (Wojtkiewicz et al., 2017b)

**Mechanism:**
Zinc signaling may exert an impact on synaptic plasticity in the peripheral nervous system, particularly in the ENS. Zinc is essential for the proper development and maintenance of the gastrointestinal system (Vela et al., 2015).

**Future Directions:**
Further investigations are necessary to clarify the precise physiological roles of ZnT3 and synaptic vesicles in the ENS across multiple species, enhancing our understanding of the mechanisms linking zinc with ASD.

## 7. MECHANISMS LINKING ENS DEFICITS TO ASD

### 7.1 Serotonergic Signaling Disruption

5-HT (serotonin) is critical for both brain and gut development. ASD-associated mutations in SLC6A4 (SERT) and other genes affect:

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Reduced serotonin-positive enteroendocrine cells (Shank3 models)
- Abnormal 5-HT clearance (SERT models)
- ENS hypoplasia (SERT Ala56 mice)
- Impaired peristalsis
- Slow GI transit

**Brain Manifestations:**
- Hyperserotoninemia (~30% of ASD children)
- Altered brain development
- Social and behavioral deficits

**Mechanism:** 5-HT signaling regulates enteric neuronal development and gastrointestinal motility. Disruption of this pathway affects both the ENS and the brain, linking GI dysfunction to ASD symptomatology.

### 7.2 Inhibitory Pathway Dysfunction

Aberrant nitrergic neurotransmission affects the balance of excitation and inhibition in the ENS:

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Increased nNOS+ neurons or varicosities
- Elevated NO levels in the ENS
- Increased nNOS expression in enteric neurons
- Aberrant innervation of GI smooth muscle by nNOS neurons

**Mechanism:** Elevated NO levels can alter smooth muscle relaxation patterns, disrupting coordinated peristalsis and leading to GI dysmotility.

**Genetic Examples:**
- NL3R451C mice show increased nNOS myenteric neurons (Hosie et al., 2019)
- Dysfunctional inhibitory pathways affect the coordination of contraction and relaxation

### 7.3 GABA Receptor Pathway Modulation

GABA signaling is critical for controlling neuronal excitability in both the brain and the ENS:

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Altered sensitivity to GABA receptor modulators
- Changes in abundance and/or composition of GABA receptors
- Altered GABAergic neurotransmission in colonic enteric neurons

**Mechanism:** Activation of GABA and GABA receptors may contribute to gastrointestinal motility, including the modulation of sensitivity toward GABA receptor modulators.

**Genetic Examples:**
- NL3R451C mice show heightened sensitivity to GABA receptor modulation in colonic enteric neurons (Hosie et al., 2019)

### 7.4 Enteric Glial Dysfunction

Enteric glial cells are now recognized as active participants in gut physiology:

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Altered glial cell numbers and morphology
- Impaired glial support and signaling
- Disrupted interactions with epithelial and immune cells

**Mechanisms:**
- Modulate synaptic signaling
- Regulate gut motility patterns
- Maintain intestinal barrier integrity (Seguella and Gulbransen, 2021)
- Interact with immune cells, mediating neuroimmune communication

**Impact:** Dysfunctional glia may contribute to both GI symptoms and systemic inflammation, potentially affecting brain function through the gut–brain axis.

### 7.5 Excitatory/Inhibitory Imbalance

ASD is characterized by excitation/inhibition imbalance in the brain. The same imbalance may occur in the ENS:

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Altered ratios of excitatory (vAChT+, ChAT+) to inhibitory (nNOS+, VIP+) neurons
- Changes in neurotransmitter levels (acetylcholine, nitric oxide, VIP)
- Disrupted coordination of contraction and relaxation

**Mechanism:** Imbalance in excitatory and inhibitory inputs disrupts the coordinated patterns of GI motility, leading to dysmotility and other GI symptoms.

**Genetic Examples:**
- Shank3 mutations affecting synaptic signaling
- NLGN3 mutations affecting synaptic maturation
- SLC6A4 mutations affecting neurotransmitter clearance

### 7.6 Intestinal Barrier Dysfunction

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Elevated zonulin-1 levels (marker of intestinal permeability)
- Increased inflammatory markers (IL-6)
- Altered GI morphology
- Changes in tight junction proteins

**Mechanisms:**
- Compromised barrier integrity allows bacterial products and toxins to enter the bloodstream
- Systemic inflammation can affect brain function
- Altered immune signaling through the gut–brain axis

**Genetic Examples:**
- Shank3-/- mice show elevated zonulin-1 and IL-6 levels (Sauer et al., 2019)

### 7.7 Gut Microbiota Dysbiosis

**ENS Manifestations:**
- Altered bacterial diversity and abundance
- Changes in microbial metabolites (SCFAs, neurotransmitters)
- Impaired microbial–ENS interactions

**Mechanisms:**
- The gut microbiota influences the functional maturation of enteric wiring (Obata and Pachnis, 2016)
- Microbial metabolites (SCFAs, serotonin, GABA, dopamine) affect ENS development and function
- Dysbiosis can lead to intestinal barrier dysfunction and systemic inflammation

**Impact:** Altered microbial composition in ASD affects ENS function, which in turn affects GI motility and signaling to the brain through the gut–brain axis.

## 8. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

### 8.1 Diagnostic Implications

GI symptoms in ASD are not merely secondary or incidental. They may serve as:

**8.1.1 Biomarkers for ASD Risk**
- Elevated whole-blood serotonin in ~30% of ASD children (Schain and Freedman, 1961; Gabriele et al., 2014)
- Gut microbiota composition differences
- Specific patterns of GI symptoms (constipation, diarrhea, abdominal pain)

**8.1.2 Subtype Identification**
- Specific genetic subtypes (SHANK3-related, NLGN3-related, CHD8-related) show distinct GI symptom profiles
- GI symptoms may predict the presence of specific genetic mutations

**8.1.3 Behavioral Correlation**
- GI dysfunction correlates with more severe social withdrawal and irritability (Chaidez et al., 2014; Madra et al., 2020)
- Patients with GI symptoms tend to experience more severe ASD behavioral symptoms

**8.1.4 Early Detection**
- GI issues often precede or accompany ASD diagnosis
- Recognition of GI symptoms may facilitate earlier ASD screening

### 8.2 Clinical Presentation of GI Symptoms in ASD

| Symptom | Prevalence in ASD | Comparison to Neurotypical |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Constipation | 40-60% | 3-4× higher |
| Diarrhea | 20-30% | 2-3× higher |
| Abdominal pain | 30-50% | 3× higher |
| GERD/Gastroesophageal reflux | 20-25% | 2× higher |
| Fecal incontinence | 10-20% | Variable |
| Food selectivity | 50-70% | 5-10× higher |

### 8.3 Assessment Considerations

**Challenges:**
- Communication difficulties in ASD patients (non-verbal or limited verbal ability)
- Atypical pain presentation
- Co-occurring sensory processing issues
- Food selectivity affecting dietary assessment

**Recommendations:**
- Use of validated GI symptom scales for ASD
- Multidisciplinary approach (gastroenterology, psychiatry, nutrition)
- Objective measures when possible (imaging, endoscopy, biomarker testing)
- Consideration of the whole patient, including behavioral and environmental factors

## 9. THERAPEUTIC STRATEGIES

### 9.1 Overview

Current research is exploring various approaches to address ENS dysfunction and GI symptoms in ASD, targeting the gut–brain axis at multiple levels.

### 9.2 Microbiome-Targeted Therapies

**9.2.1 Probiotics**

| Agent | Mechanism | Evidence |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| Lactobacillus species | Modulate gut microbiota; produce SCFAs; enhance barrier function | Mixed results in human studies; promising in animal models |
| Bifidobacterium species | Modulate gut microbiota; produce SCFAs; anti-inflammatory effects | Promising in animal models; limited human studies |
| Saccharomyces boulardii | Probiotic yeast; modulates immune function | Limited studies in ASD |

**9.2.2 Prebiotics**
- Non-digestible fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria
- Examples: fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS)
- Evidence: Limited studies in ASD

**9.2.3 Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)**
- Transfer of healthy donor microbiota to restore balance
- Evidence: Emerging; small studies show improvement in both GI and ASD symptoms
- Challenges: Long-term safety, standardization

**9.2.4 Microbiota Transfer Therapy (MTT)**
- Comprehensive protocol including antibiotics, bowel cleanse, and FMT
- Evidence: Initial studies show sustained improvement in GI and ASD symptoms
- Promising but requires further investigation

### 9.3 Dietary Interventions

**9.3.1 Gluten-Free/Casein-Free (GFCF) Diet**
- Eliminates gluten (wheat) and casein (dairy)
- Rationale: Some patients may have sensitivity to these proteins
- Evidence: Mixed; some studies show improvement in GI and behavioral symptoms; others show no benefit
- Challenges: Nutritional adequacy, adherence

**9.3.2 Ketogenic Diet**
- High-fat, low-carbohydrate diet
- Rationale: May improve energy metabolism, reduce inflammation
- Evidence: Limited studies in ASD

**9.3.3 Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD)**
- Eliminates complex carbohydrates and starches
- Rationale: Reduces fermentable substrates for pathogenic bacteria
- Evidence: Limited; some parental reports of improvement

**9.3.4 Elimination Diets**
- Removal of specific foods suspected of causing symptoms
- Rationale: Identify and eliminate triggers
- Evidence: Case reports and small studies

### 9.4 Zinc Supplementation

**Rationale:**
- Zinc deficiency is more prevalent in ASD
- Zinc is essential for ENS development and function
- ZnT3 and zinc modulate enteric neurotransmission

**Evidence:**
- Preclinical studies show promise
- Limited clinical trials in ASD

**Considerations:**
- Dosage and form (zinc picolinate, zinc citrate, zinc gluconate)
- Copper balance (excess zinc can deplete copper)
- Individual variability in response

### 9.5 Neuroprotective and Neuroregenerative Approaches

**9.5.1 Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS)**
- Modulates gut–brain communication
- Targets the vagus nerve, the primary neural pathway between the ENS and the brain
- Evidence: Investigational for both GI symptoms and ASD symptoms
- Mechanisms: Modulates inflammation, neurotransmitter release, neuronal plasticity

**9.5.2 Targeted Pharmacotherapy**

| Target | Agent | Rationale | Evidence |
|--------|-------|-----------|----------|
| SERT/SLC6A4 | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) | Regulate serotonin levels in gut and brain | Mixed results; needs further study |
| SHANK3 | IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor-1) | Potential neuroregenerative effects | Preclinical studies |
| GABA receptors | GABA agonists/modulators | Restore excitation/inhibition balance | Preclinical studies |
| Nitrergic pathway | NO modulators | Normalize inhibitory neurotransmission | Preclinical studies |
| 5-HT receptors | 5-HT receptor agonists/antagonists | Modulate 5-HT signaling | Preclinical studies |

### 9.6 Behavioral and Psychological Interventions

**9.6.1 Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**
- Address anxiety related to GI symptoms
- Improve coping strategies
- Address pain perception

**9.6.2 Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)**
- Address food selectivity
- Improve mealtime behaviors
- Address toileting difficulties

**9.6.3 Sensory Integration Therapy**
- Address sensory issues related to eating and digestion
- May improve food acceptance

**9.6.4 Parent Education and Support**
- Educate parents about GI symptoms and management
- Reduce stress and improve quality of life

### 9.7 Emerging Therapies

**9.7.1 CRISPR/Cas9 Gene Editing**
- Potential to correct ASD-associated genetic mutations
- Challenges: Delivery, off-target effects, ethical considerations

**9.7.2 Stem Cell Therapy**
- Potential to replace damaged enteric neurons
- Challenges: Safety, efficacy, ethical considerations

**9.7.3 Enteric Glial Cell Therapies**
- Target glial dysfunction
- Challenges: Still in early research stages

**9.7.4 Microbial Engineering**
- Design of synthetic microbial consortia
- Delivery of therapeutic molecules
- Challenges: Safety, efficacy, regulatory approval

|----------|----------|-------------------|
| Does early ENS intervention prevent ASD? | High | Prospective cohort studies, early intervention trials |
| Can GI symptoms predict ASD severity? | High | Longitudinal studies |

| How does diet affect ENS function in ASD? | Moderate | Controlled dietary studies |
| What is the role of zinc in ENS development? | Moderate | Mechanistic studies |
| Can biomarkers predict treatment response? | Low to Moderate | Clinical trials with biomarker stratification |

- Patient-derived enteric organoids
- Drug screening platforms

**10.2.2 Multi-Omics Integration**
- Genomics
- Transcriptomics
- Proteomics
- Metabolomics
- Microbiomics

**10.2.3 Neuroimaging**
- Functional MRI of gut-brain axis

- Brain connectivity studies

**10.2.4 Biomarker Development**

### 10.3 Methodological Challenges

| Challenge | Description | Potential Solutions |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| Sample Size | Many studies have small sample sizes | Large-scale, multicenter studies |
| Dietary Controls | Dietary patterns affect microbiota composition | Controlled dietary studies |
| Regional Disparities | Geographic and cultural differences | Standardized protocols across sites |

| Communication Barriers | Difficulty assessing GI symptoms in non-verbal individuals | Validated instruments, objective measures |
| Comorbidities | Co-occurring conditions affect outcomes | Stratified analyses |

## 11. CONCLUSION

The enteric nervous system plays a far more significant role in autism spectrum disorder than previously recognized. Structural and functional deficits in the ENS contribute to the high prevalence of GI symptoms in ASD and may directly influence core behavioral features through the gut–brain axis.

### 11.1 Key Findings

1. **ENS Deficits are Intrinsic to ASD:**
   - Structural abnormalities (reduced neuronal density, altered neuronal populations, glial dysfunction)
   - Functional impairments (dysmotility, impaired secretion, barrier dysfunction)
   - Observed across multiple genetic models and patient populations

2. **Genetic Risk Factors Affect Both Brain and ENS:**
   - ASD-associated genes (SHANK3, NLGN3, CNTNAP2, SLC6A4, CHD8) are expressed in both the brain and the ENS
   - Mutations in these genes affect both neurological development and gastrointestinal function
   - The genetic architecture of ASD may simultaneously affect both systems

3. **Environmental Factors Compound Genetic Risk:**
   - Prenatal factors (maternal immune activation)
   - Gut microbiota dysbiosis
   - Zinc deficiency
   - These factors affect ENS development and function

4. **Multiple Mechanisms Link ENS Deficits to ASD:**
   - Serotonergic signaling disruption
   - Inhibitory pathway dysfunction
   - GABA receptor pathway modulation
   - Enteric glial dysfunction
   - Excitatory/inhibitory imbalance
   - Intestinal barrier dysfunction
   - Gut microbiota dysbiosis

5. **Therapeutic Implications are Significant:**
   - Microbiome-targeted therapies
   - Dietary interventions
   - Zinc supplementation
   - Neuroprotective approaches
   - Behavioral and psychological interventions

### 11.2 Clinical and Research Implications

Understanding the ENS–ASD connection opens new avenues for:

**Earlier Diagnosis:**
- GI symptoms may serve as early biomarkers for ASD
- Recognition of specific GI patterns may facilitate earlier screening

**Subtype Identification:**
- Specific genetic subtypes show distinct GI symptom profiles
- GI symptoms may predict the presence of specific genetic mutations

**Novel Therapeutic Strategies:**
- Targeting the gut–brain axis
- Microbiome-based interventions
- Neuroprotective approaches addressing both ENS and CNS dysfunction

**Personalized Medicine:**
- Tailored interventions based on genetic subtype and GI symptom profile
- Integration of GI management into ASD care

### 11.3 Final Thoughts

As research continues, the "second brain" may prove to be as important as the first in understanding, treating, and potentially preventing autism spectrum disorder. The recognition that ENS dysfunction is not merely a comorbidity but an integral part of ASD pathophysiology represents a paradigm shift with significant clinical and research implications.

The challenge ahead lies in translating these findings into effective therapies and improving the quality of life for individuals with ASD and their families. This requires:
- Large-scale, well-designed studies
- Integration of multiple disciplines (gastroenterology, neurology, psychiatry, nutrition, microbiology)
- Patient and family engagement
- Translation of research findings into clinical practice

The future of ASD research and treatment lies in understanding the whole nervous system—both the first brain and the second brain—and the bidirectional communication between them.
